My mind-body approach

Your symptoms are real, and your brain plays a big part in them.

When stress or pain lasts, the nervous system's alarm can get stuck on. Your brain can learn new patterns, and we get there in three stages.

Photo by Cheng Shi Song on Pexels

01

Understand

I explain what's happening in your brain and body in plain language, so your symptoms make sense.

02

Make room

Using ACT, mindfulness and EMDR, you learn to make room for discomfort instead of fighting it.

03

Rebuild

Small, values-guided steps build new patterns until they become your new default.

Each time you practice a new response, you strengthen a new path in your brain. Over time, it becomes the easier one to take.

Pain is the brain's protective alarm, not a direct measure of damage. One famous case shows this clearly.

What happened. In 1995, the British Medical Journal described a 29-year-old builder who jumped down onto a 15 cm (6-inch) nail. It went straight through his boot. He was in so much pain that the emergency team gave him strong pain medication before carefully removing the boot. Underneath, they found the nail had passed between his toes. His foot was completely uninjured.

What it shows. His pain was real. But it came from his brain's judgment of danger, not from damage. The brain decides how much something hurts based on what we see, expect, and fear, not only on what is happening in our tissues.

Why it matters for you. The same alarm can stay switched on. When stress, worry, or past pain keeps it on high alert, it can keep sounding long after the body has healed. That's why we work with the brain: an alarm that has learned to be overprotective can also learn to settle.

Source: Fisher et al., 1995, British Medical Journal (via PainScience)

Couples therapy

When stress comes home, your relationship feels it too.

Stress and pain rarely stay with one person. They show up as short tempers, distance, or the same argument on repeat.

I especially love working with young couples, including interracial and international couples navigating two cultures, two families, and sometimes two languages.

I'm trained in the Gottman Method, an approach to couples therapy built on five decades of relationship research by Drs. John and Julie Gottman.

How the Gottman Method works

01

Get a clear picture

We start with a joint session, a session with each of you, and a research-based questionnaire to map your relationship's strengths and challenges.

02

Rebuild friendship

You reconnect through small, everyday moments: knowing each other's world, sharing appreciation, and turning toward each other.

03

Fight differently

You learn to spot four patterns that research links to relationship trouble (criticism, contempt, defensiveness and stonewalling) and replace them with gentler ways to talk.

Where the brain-body work comes in. When an argument heats up, your body can flood with stress, and it becomes hard to really listen. We practice noticing that moment and calming down together, so you can come back and hear each other.
