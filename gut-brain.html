The gut-brain axis

Why stress goes straight to your stomach

If anxiety shows up as stomach pain, reflux, nausea, or urgent trips to the bathroom, you are not imagining it. Your gut and your brain are in constant conversation, and stress changes what they say to each other.

What is the gut-brain axis?

The gut-brain axis is the two-way communication system between your digestive tract and your brain. The vagus nerve is the main link between them. Your gut also has its own network of more than 500 million neurons, the enteric nervous system, which is sometimes called the "second brain."

Hormones, the immune system, and the trillions of microbes living in your gut are part of this conversation too. Signals travel in both directions: your brain affects your digestion, and your gut sends a steady stream of information back to your brain.

How stress affects your digestion

When your body senses threat, it shifts out of "rest and digest" mode and into "fight or flight." Digestion is not a priority in an emergency, so stress can change how food moves through your gut and how sensitive your gut feels.

Emotions can also make gut sensations feel more intense. This can create a loop many people know well:

  • Stress or worry makes your stomach act up.
  • The symptoms themselves cause more worry, and you start scanning your body for the next flare.
  • That extra attention and alarm makes the gut even more sensitive, and the cycle continues.

IBS is a disorder of gut-brain interaction

Gastroenterologists now describe irritable bowel syndrome as "a chronic, often debilitating, and highly prevalent disorder of gut-brain interaction." Conditions like IBS used to be called "functional" disorders. The newer name reflects what research shows: these symptoms involve how the gut and brain communicate.

This does not mean the problem is "all in your head." It means your nervous system is part of the picture, and that gives you more ways to feel better.

What the research says about therapy

The American College of Gastroenterology suggests gut-directed psychotherapies, such as cognitive behavioral therapy, mindfulness, and hypnosis, for overall IBS symptoms, while noting that the quality of the evidence is still limited. A large 2020 review of psychological therapies for IBS found that CBT-based approaches and gut-directed hypnotherapy had the most evidence, in both the short and long term.

Counseling does not replace medical care for your gut. It works alongside it, by helping the brain side of the gut-brain axis.

How I can help

I know this connection personally. Stress once showed up in my own body as reflux and gut pain, and counseling was a turning point in my healing. In our work together, we can:

  • Map your gut-brain loop: what tends to set off symptoms, and what happens in your body and mind next.
  • Use mindfulness and acceptance-based tools to notice gut sensations without spiraling into fear.
  • Gently reduce avoidance, like skipping meals, plans, or travel because of symptoms.
  • Work on the stress behind the symptoms, such as perfectionism, relationships, or the pressures of living between cultures.

Please see a doctor about new, worsening, or concerning digestive symptoms, such as blood in your stool, unintended weight loss, trouble swallowing, or symptoms that wake you at night. Counseling works best alongside a medical evaluation.
